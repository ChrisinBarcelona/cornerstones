# 01. Bias: Optimism Bias

Optimism bias is a cognitive bias characterized by individuals' tendency to overestimate the likelihood of positive outcomes and underestimate the likelihood of negative outcomes. It’s also referenced as **delusional optimism.** Research and evidence suggests that the bias is stronger for negative events (the [*valence effect*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_effect)) than positive outcomes. Several factors contribute to the development and persistence of optimism bias:

## How it works

1. **Wishful Thinking**: People tend to focus on finding information that supports what they want to see happen and therefore they may engage in wishful thinking, imagining positive outcomes they desire while downplaying or ignoring potential obstacles or risks. This bias can lead individuals to overestimate the likelihood of achieving their desired outcomes.
2. **Overconfidence**: Optimism bias is often associated with overconfidence in one's abilities, judgments, or predictions. Individuals may believe that they are better equipped to handle challenges or achieve success than they objectively are, leading to inflated expectations.
3. **Desire for Control**: Optimism bias may stem from the innate human desire for control over one's life and circumstances. Believing that positive outcomes are more likely can give individuals a sense of agency and influence over their future.

---

Optimism Bias is closely associated with overconfidence bias and it’s important to recognise that generally, whilst everything will be fine, and it’s good to be optimistic, in business, it’s important to recognise that objective analyse of a situation will serve you better than an inert belief that, without work, things will turn out great!

## Why it matters for UX

Optimism bias makes teams underestimate how long things take and overestimate how much users will love a feature. Plan with buffers, test early, and design for things going wrong: errors, empty states and failed payments are part of the experience.

## Try it: spot it in the wild (20 minutes)

1. Estimate how long your next project task will take, then write it down.
2. List three things that could go wrong with it.
3. Compare your estimate with the real time when you finish, and note the difference.

- We overestimate good outcomes and underestimate bad ones.
- Plan buffers into timelines and test assumptions early.
- Design for failure: error, empty and offline states matter.
