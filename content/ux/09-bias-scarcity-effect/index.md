# 09. Bias: Scarcity Effect

The scarcity effect, also known as the scarcity principle, refers to the psychological phenomenon where people place a higher value on items or opportunities that are perceived to be scarce or limited in availability.

## How it works

![1_KccPY8Hk3MVx07XyCMRVnQ.jpg](1_KccPY8Hk3MVx07XyCMRVnQ.jpg)

When something is scarce, it creates a sense of urgency and fear of missing out (FOMO) in individuals. This can lead to increased desire and motivation to obtain the item or opportunity. The scarcity effect can influence various aspects of human behavior, including decision-making, purchasing behavior, and perception of value.

---

The scarcity effect can be attributed to several underlying psychological factors. One factor is the concept of reactance, which suggests that people have a natural aversion to restrictions on their freedom. When something becomes scarce, the perceived restriction triggers a desire to regain the freedom or access that is being limited.

![download-1.jpg](download-1.jpg)

---

Additionally, scarcity often serves as a heuristic, a mental shortcut, for assessing the value and quality of something. If an item is scarce, people tend to infer that it must be more valuable or superior in some way, even without concrete evidence. This heuristic is driven by the belief that others desire the scarce item as well, creating social proof and increasing its perceived worth.

---

Marketers and salespeople often utilize the scarcity effect as a persuasive technique. Creating a perception of limited availability or time-limited offers can enhance the perceived value of a product or service, and prompt individuals to take immediate action to avoid missing out.

It's important to note that the scarcity effect can lead to irrational decision-making and impulse buying if individuals don't critically evaluate the actual value and necessity of the scarce item.

---

## Why it matters for UX

"Only 2 left" and "Offer ends tonight" are scarcity cues, and they work. When they are true they help users act in time; when they are fake (endless countdowns, invented stock levels) they are deceptive patterns that erode trust and may break consumer law.

## Try it: spot it in the wild (20 minutes)

1. Find three scarcity messages on shopping or travel sites.
2. Reload each page later or the next day. Is the message still the same?
3. Decide whether each is honest, and redesign one dishonest example.

## Key takeaways

- We value what is rare or about to disappear.
- Real scarcity cues help users act in time; fake ones are deceptive patterns.
- Trust lost to fake urgency is hard to win back.
