## Symmetry and Beauty in Nature

Beauty is synonymous with symmetry and symmetry is an extension of the physical world. Your work must achieve a cadence and consistency which gives the user a pattern they can predict.

It's important that our work is beautiful, so that it is **perceived** as good and beneficial.

## The Attractiveness Halo

"Beauty" is subjective and shaped by culture, so be careful with the idea of "beautiful people". What researchers can measure is how people are **rated**: and people who are *rated* as more attractive tend to be judged more favourably on unrelated qualities, such as competence, honesty or intelligence. Psychologists call this the **halo effect**.

- **Judgements:** a large review of the research (Langlois and colleagues, 2000, covering more than 900 studies) found that people rated as attractive are judged and treated more positively, by strangers and by people who know them.
- **Earnings:** in a well-known study of the labour market, the economists Hamermesh and Biddle (1994) found a measurable pay gap linked to how attractive people were rated.

![chriskellylive_Create_a_photorealistic_image_of_a_business_woma_52155577-c0ef-472c-9ba8-bf604a352f14.png](4073e146-235b-4150-bec8-321fda968575.png)

None of this is fair, and it says more about our biases than about people. But it matters for design, because the same halo applies to things: what looks attractive is assumed to work better. That's why aesthetics forms a core usability heuristic.

## The Beauty Heuristic (Heuristic 8)

Aesthetically pleasing designs can provide memorable experiences that differentiate a brand. However, interfaces should only include necessary elements, with high informational value. Clarity will always win over visual flourish.

> Have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful.
> 

William Morris

Interfaces should not contain information which is irrelevant or rarely needed. Every extra unit of information in an interface competes with the relevant units of information and diminishes their relative visibility.

## Balancing Form with Function

> Form follows function.
> 

Louis Sullivan

Is the famous maxim coined by architect Louis Sullivan (1856–1924), and through this idea he articulates the principle that the shape of a building or object should directly relate to its intended function or purpose. 

Whilst the aesthetic will earn you initial trust from the user, without substance they will quickly abandon your software.

## Summary

Beauty earns trust: people treat attractive things, and attractive interfaces, as better and more credible. Use that. Make your work beautiful, consistent and simple, but never let style replace substance. Form follows function, every element must earn its place, and beauty should never be used to hide a bad deal.

> Appearances are often deceiving.

Aesop

## Why it matters for UX

Users judge beautiful interfaces as easier to use and more trustworthy, even when they aren't (the aesthetic-usability effect). That gives good visual design real power: it buys patience and trust. It also means beauty can hide usability problems, so test with users rather than trusting first impressions.

## Try it: beauty versus usability (25 minutes)

1. Find two apps that do the same job, one you find beautiful and one you find plain.
2. Complete the same task in both and time yourself.
3. Note where the beautiful one was harder to use than it looked, and where the plain one was easier.
4. Restyle one screen of the plain app to make it more attractive, keeping every function, and test which one people trust more.
