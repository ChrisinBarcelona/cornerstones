# 04. Bias: Bandwagon Effect

The Bandwagon Effect, also known as herd mentality or groupthink, is a cognitive bias in which people tend to adopt or conform to a popular belief, trend, or behavior simply because many others are doing so. It's the idea that the more people adopt a particular idea or behavior, the more likely others are to follow suit, regardless of the merits or validity of the idea itself.

## How it works

![](https://images.prismic.io/sketchplanations/42f5c453-6ad5-4e66-a19d-e95ec3e00200_SP+703+-+The+bandwagon+effect.png?auto=compress,format)

---

The bandwagon effect is driven by social influence and the desire to fit in, gain social approval, or avoid social rejection. It can occur in various contexts, such as consumer behavior, fashion trends, political opinions, and even decision-making processes.

For example, in a political context, individuals may support a candidate or political party simply because they perceive that candidate or party as being popular or having momentum in the polls. They may align their views with the majority without thoroughly examining the policies or qualifications of the candidate.

---

---

The bandwagon effect can have both positive and negative implications. On one hand, it can facilitate the spread of beneficial ideas, innovations, and behaviors through social influence. On the other hand, it can lead to conformity, uncritical thinking, and a lack of independent judgment.

To mitigate the bandwagon effect, it is important for individuals to critically evaluate information, consider diverse perspectives, and make decisions based on rational analysis rather than solely relying on the popularity or consensus of others. It's important to think independently and avoid succumbing to the pressure of conformity solely because "everyone else is doing it."

---

## Why it matters for UX

People follow what others do, so social proof (reviews, ratings, "most popular", user counts) is one of the most powerful patterns in UX. Used honestly, it helps users decide; faked (invented counts, "12 people are looking at this"), it is a deceptive pattern. In your team, popularity is also not proof that an idea is right.

## Try it: spot it in the wild (20 minutes)

1. Find three examples of social proof in apps you use.
2. For each, decide whether you can tell it is real.
3. Redesign one so it shows more honest, more useful proof (for example, recent reviews from similar users).

## Key takeaways

- Honest social proof helps users decide; fake proof is deceptive.
- In design decisions, popularity is not evidence.
