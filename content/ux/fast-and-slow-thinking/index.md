# Fast and Slow Thinking

How people really make decisions, and why it changes how you design.

---

## Kahneman and behavioural economics

In 2002, the psychologist **Daniel Kahneman** was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work, much of it with Amos Tversky, on how people make judgements and decisions under uncertainty. Their work helped found **behavioural economics**.

![An awards ceremony](chriskellylive_an_awards_ceremony_in_the_swiss_alps_fantastic_l_8cd04db0-65af-4d49-8bf5-38cbe740f765.jpg)

His central finding was that people don't make decisions the way classical economics assumed. We are not perfectly rational. Years of experiments showed that our minds use two modes of thinking, which Kahneman called **System 1** and **System 2** and described in his book *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).

---

## System 1: fast thinking

System 1 works automatically and quickly, with little or no effort. It recognises patterns, recalls memories and jumps to conclusions. Try these:

1. Complete the phrase: bread and …
2. What's 2 + 2?
3. What's the first letter of the word "Bing"?

You didn't have to try. That's System 1: quick, intuitive and effortless, but prone to bias and error.

---

## System 2: slow thinking

System 2 is deliberate. It allocates attention to effortful mental work: calculations, comparisons and careful reasoning. Try these:

1. What is 17 × 24?
2. Tell me about the last time you heard a surprising sound.
3. How many i's are in the word "indivisibilities"?

You felt the effort. System 2 is slow, tiring and easily overloaded, but it's more accurate.

## What it means for design

Kahneman gave designers something close to a manual for how people decide. Two lessons follow:

- **For your users: design for System 1.** Most of the time, people use products on autopilot. Familiar patterns, clear hierarchy and low cognitive load let them act without thinking hard. Don't make users think. When a decision genuinely matters (a payment, a deletion, sharing personal data), deliberately slow them down and let System 2 check it.
- **For you: use System 2.** Designers fall for System 1 too: first ideas, gut feelings and "it looks good". Don't rush into Figma. Slow the process down, question your assumptions and let research and testing lead to accurate, evidence-based results.

The same knowledge can be misused: designing to keep people on autopilot so they agree to things they'd reject if they stopped to think. See [Design Ethics and User Coercion](design-ethics.html).

---

## Why it matters for UX

Users make most decisions quickly and intuitively, so the easiest design to use is one that works with fast thinking: familiar, clear and low effort. Knowing when to slow people down, and slowing yourself down as a designer, is what separates thoughtful design from guesswork.

## Try it: fast or slow? (20 minutes)

1. Pick a checkout or sign-up flow in an app you use.
2. Mark every step as System 1 (automatic) or System 2 (needs thought).
3. Find one step that makes people think when it shouldn't, and redesign it to be effortless.
4. Find one step that should slow people down (a payment, a deletion, a consent) and check that it does.

## Key takeaways

- System 1 is fast, automatic and biased; System 2 is slow, effortful and accurate.
- Design everyday tasks for fast thinking, and slow users down only for decisions that matter.
- As a designer, use slow thinking: research and test instead of trusting your first idea.
