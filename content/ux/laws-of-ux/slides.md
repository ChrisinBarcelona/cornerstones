## Perception
How people group what they see.
---
@split
## Law of Proximity

![The CNN website footer: dozens of links in columns](Screenshot_2024-05-25_at_23.18.46.png)

Things **close together** seem to belong together.

How many groups do you see in CNN's footer? Nobody drew a box. Space did it.
---
@split
## Law of Common Region

![A dark app-store screen with extensions grouped inside rounded panels](screen.png)

Things inside a **shared boundary** seem grouped.

A card, a panel or a background colour tells users "these go together".
---
@split
## Law of Similarity

![Law of Similarity poster: light and dark dots in a grid](law-of-similarity_poster.jpg)

Things that **look alike** seem related, even when they're far apart.

Make every link look like a link, and nothing else.
---
@split
## Law of Prägnanz

![A simple, geometric monogram logo](3eeacecbecb5942be8bb38619bb83f05.jpg)

People see complex or ambiguous shapes in the **simplest form** possible.

Keep icons and logos simple, so they're read in an instant.

@section
## Decisions and memory
How people choose and remember.
---
@split
## Hick's Law

![An iPhone call-waiting screen: End & Accept, Decline, Hold & Accept](cedb9958-345f-4fad-9ae2-f8053bbc98be.png)

The more choices, and the more complex they are, the **longer it takes to decide**.

Three similar buttons, in a hurry, with your phone ringing. Keep choices few and clear.
---
@text
## Miller's Law

The average person can keep only about **seven (plus or minus two)** items in working memory.

Don't design to the number. Design for **chunking**: break information into small, meaningful groups, like a phone number written 612 345 678.
---
@split
## Von Restorff Effect

![YouTube's mobile app with one bright blue Install button among the content](IMG_0149.jpg)

When several similar things appear together, the one that **differs** is the one people remember.

What stands out here? Is it the most important thing on the screen, or an advert?
---
@cards
## More laws of memory and experience

- **Serial position** People remember the first and last items best: onboarding and endings matter
- **Peak-end rule** We judge an experience by its peak and its end. Make both great
- **Zeigarnik effect** Unfinished tasks stick in the mind: progress bars, "profile 80% complete"
- **Aesthetic-usability** Beautiful designs are perceived as easier to use

@section
## Familiarity and complexity
Work with what people know.
---
@quote
> Users spend most of their time on other sites.

**Jakob's Law.** They are the rule. You are the exception. Use the established language of the web.
---
@cards
## Two more for your toolkit

- **Tesler's Law** Every system has complexity that can't be removed. Remove everything else, and make the system carry the rest, not the user
- **Pareto principle** Roughly 80% of use comes from 20% of features. Make that 20% excellent

## Check yourself

1. **Hick's Law:** fewer choices, faster decisions
2. **Von Restorff Effect:** the one that differs is noticed
3. **Zeigarnik Effect:** an unfinished task pulls people to finish
4. **Peak-end rule** (and serial position): the ending shapes the memory
5. **Jakob's Law:** match what people already know
6. **Miller's Law:** chunking
