@title
# Design Ethics
Influence is the job. Honesty is the choice.
---
@statement
Gestalt, colour, bias, fast thinking: everything on this course is a way of **influencing people**.

It can make products easier and fairer. It can also push people into things they never meant to do.
---
@split
## Two pressures

![A slot machine paying out cherries and dollars](chriskellylive_a_slot_machine_with_three_cherries_wcash_dollars_40d0e7df-4b55-40e6-a9f7-0b4280aaf3ca.jpg)

**One:** your employer will judge you on engagement, retention and conversion.

**Two:** the people on the other side of the screen respond to rewards. Likes, streaks, notifications and infinite scroll keep them coming back.
---
@statement
**User coercion** is design that pressures people into actions that benefit the business more than the user.

These are **deceptive patterns**, also called dark patterns.
---
@cards
## Deceptive patterns

- **Forced continuity** A free trial quietly becomes a paid subscription
- **Roach motel** Easy to join, deliberately hard to leave
- **Hidden costs** Fees that appear only at the last step
- **Confirmshaming** "No thanks, I don't like saving money"
- **False urgency** Fake countdowns and "only 2 left!"
- **Sneaking** Extras added to your basket without asking
---
@split
## Forced continuity

![A finger about to press a red Free Trial key on a keyboard](iStock-883005738.jpg)

The trial ends, the payments start, often without clear notice.

Then the cancel option is hidden behind step after step. It traps people into payments they never really agreed to, and it destroys trust.

@text
## The cost: real people

Common Sense Media's 2021 census found that US children aged 8 to 12 spent an average of **5 hours 33 minutes a day** on screen media for entertainment, up from 4 hours 44 minutes in 2019.

Heavy, compulsive phone use has been linked with poorer sleep, higher anxiety and lower life satisfaction among young people. Our patterns are part of that system.
---
@text
## Data can be used against people

In 2018, the whistleblower Christopher Wylie revealed that **Cambridge Analytica** had harvested data from millions of Facebook profiles without consent, and used it to target political advertising.

What you collect about users is a **responsibility**, not just a resource.
---
@cards
## The law is catching up

- **EU** The Digital Services Act bans platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users
- **US** The FTC sued Amazon in 2023 over Prime sign-ups and cancellation
- **2025** Amazon agreed to pay **$2.5 billion** to settle, and to make cancelling Prime clear and simple
---
@cards
## Ask of every screen

- **Informed** Would they make the same choice if they fully understood it?
- **Symmetric** Is saying no as easy as saying yes?
- **Visible** Is anything hidden, pre-ticked or deliberately confusing?
- **Public** Would I be comfortable seeing this explained on the news?
- **Fair** Does it serve the user, or only the metric?

@exercise
## Name the pattern

1. "No thanks, I'd rather pay full price"
2. A €4.99 "service fee" appears on the final payment screen
3. Sign up in one tap; cancel by phone, weekdays 9 to 5
4. "Offer ends in 09:59", every time you reload the page
5. Travel insurance is already in your basket when you check out
6. "Start your free trial" with the €12.99 monthly price in tiny grey text

Name each one, and rewrite it honestly.
---
@answers
## Check yourself

1. **Confirmshaming.** Honest: *"No thanks"*
2. **Hidden costs.** Show all fees from the first price
3. **Roach motel.** Let people cancel online, in as many taps as it took to join
4. **False urgency.** Only show a deadline that's real
5. **Sneaking into the basket.** Offer it as an unticked option
6. **Forced continuity.** Say plainly: *"Free for 7 days, then €12.99 a month. Cancel any time"*, and remind people before they're charged
