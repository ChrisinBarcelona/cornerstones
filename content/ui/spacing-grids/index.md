# Spacing & Grids


## The Early History of Grids

It’s likely that the oldest grid system was something resembling the baseline grid: guidelines—or “helper lines”—drawn onto ancient manuscripts that aided the scribe in creating text that was straight and evenly spaced. **Simple column grids** can be found as early as in the Dead Sea Scrolls, where they serve to organize text into **readable blocks** within a long, rolled-up document.

![](https://storage.designlab.com/content-images/845a8dc6.jpg)

The Temple Scroll—one of the Dead Sea Scrolls, which date from between 150 BCE–70 CE.

Some 1500 years later, this same principle was readily transferred to early western printing press machines, which required metal blocks of “movable type” to be loaded, one letter at a time, into a series of lines, to be manually inked and then pressed onto paper.

**The Gutenberg Bible**—the first western book printed using movable type—**uses a two-column grid.**

![](https://storage.designlab.com/content-images/c926a35c.jpg)

The Gutenberg Bible, circa 1454, uses a two-column grid.

**Newspapers in** the late 19th and early 20th century expanded their use of large column grids, starting to maximise the amount of information they could fit on a sheet of newsprint.

On a large piece of paper like a broadsheet newsprint (which is about 30 inches by 23 inches), using columns means that a smaller type size could be used (often 8pt), and a short line length sustained within each column, maintaining readability. Without columns, the lines would be too long and difficult to follow.

![](https://storage.designlab.com/content-images/8c8b5dcd.jpg)

Apart from giving a starting point for creative direction, grid systems are a practical necessity

📝 They provide a **structure** for layout and **visual organization**, while simultaneously delivering a framework that developers can then follow to ensure the design behaves as intended, regardless of the device used.

## The 8-Point System

![Untitled](Untitled-2.png)

![1_SIQ-MUJ3-WBYBD8AMKd0eg.webp](1_SIQ-MUJ3-WBYBD8AMKd0eg.webp)

> *The principle of the 8pt grid is to use multiples of 8 (8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, etc.) to layout, dimensions, padding, and margin of elements.*
> 

### What are the benefits?

- **Consistent  & efficient UI**: When all of your measurements follow the same rules, you automatically get a more consistent UI.
- The **best system of communication** between designers and developers: a developer can easily understand and eyeball 8pt increment instead of having to measure each time.
- **Multi-platform design**: Regardless of form-factor, most popular screen sizes are divisible by 8 on at least one axis - usually both.
- **Faster decision making:** As a designer you won’t have to break your head if you should use 12px or 16px.

---

## Why it matters for UX

Grids and consistent spacing are what make a design look professional, and what make it buildable. A layout on an 8-point grid with clear columns and gutters is easier to read, easier to make responsive and easier for developers to build exactly as designed.

## Try it: grid up a screen (30 minutes)

1. In Figma, create an iPhone 16 frame with a 4-column layout grid (16 px margins, 16 px gutters).
2. Add an 8 px square grid on top.
3. Rebuild one screen from your project so every size, padding and gap is a multiple of 8.
4. Create a desktop frame with a 12-column grid and place the same content on it.

## Key takeaways

- Grids have organised text since the Dead Sea Scrolls; on screens they also make layouts responsive.
- Columns, gutters and margins structure the layout; the 8-point system sets every size.
- Consistent spacing makes designs look polished and easy for developers to build.
