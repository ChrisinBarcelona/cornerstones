How the company that invented the digital camera was killed by it, and why that story is the reason we design in fast, iterative sprints.

The company that had everything

For most of the 20th century, Kodak was photography. Its film, cameras and printing dominated the market, and "a Kodak moment" became an everyday phrase. At its peak in the late 1990s, the company was worth around $30 billion.

The invention it buried

In 1975, a young Kodak engineer called Steven Sasson built the first digital camera. Kodak's managers saw the threat: digital photos didn't need film, and film was where Kodak made its money. So they protected the business they had instead of building the one that was coming.

Others didn't wait. Digital cameras took over in the 2000s, then smartphones put a camera in every pocket, and photos moved to screens and social media. In January 2012, Kodak filed for bankruptcy.

The lesson isn't that Kodak lacked the technology. It had it first. It lacked the willingness to listen to how people's behaviour was changing, and to adapt.

Adapt or disappear

Kodak wasn't alone. Telecoms companies watched messaging move to WhatsApp and Instagram. Video rental stores, travel agencies, music labels, bookshops and banks all saw their markets change, or vanish, with the internet. Companies built around one specialised way of doing things struggled most.

Surviving that kind of change needs a different way of working: adaptation over specialisation. Watch how customers behave, test ideas quickly and change direction before the market does it for you. That's the mindset behind design thinking and agile methods.

Big launches versus small loops

The old way was a waterfall: have a great idea, spend the resources building it, launch it, and only then learn whether anyone wanted it.

In practice, the build always takes much longer than anyone anticipated, so the learning comes late, after the money and time are gone. Modern teams shrink that loop: build something small, test it with real people, learn and repeat. You'll see where that idea came from in Agile, Lean and the Toyota Way.

Why it matters for UX

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975 and still went bankrupt in 2012, because it protected its old business instead of following its customers. It's the classic reason companies adopt design thinking: test fast, listen to users and be ready to change direction before the market does it for you.

Try it: spot the next Kodak (20 minutes)

  1. Pick an industry you know: banking, taxis, newspapers, travel or another.
  2. List what customers used to do, and what they do now.
  3. Find one company that adapted and one that didn't, and write what each did differently.
  4. Write one question a design thinking team should be asking in that industry today.

Key takeaways

  • Kodak invented the digital camera, then went from about $30 billion to bankruptcy by protecting film.
  • Specialisation without adaptation is fatal when markets shift.
  • Small build–test–learn loops beat big launches: learn before the money runs out.
