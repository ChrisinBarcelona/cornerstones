Design is not a product, it's a feeling.

Don Norman

People don't only use products, they feel something about them. This lecture is about how design creates those feelings, and the three tools you'll use most to shape them: colour, type and brand.

Three levels of emotional design

In his book Emotional Design (2004), Don Norman describes three levels at which we respond to a design:

  • Visceral: the instant, gut reaction to how something looks, sounds and feels. "Ooh, that's beautiful."
  • Behavioural: how it feels to use: is it easy, efficient, in control? "That was so simple."
  • Reflective: what it means to us afterwards: our memories, self-image and the story we tell others. "This is the kind of product I use."

Good design works on all three. A beautiful app that's frustrating to use fails at the behavioural level; a usable one nobody is proud of fails at the reflective level.

Emotion and decision making

People decide emotionally far more often than they think. As you'll see in Fast and Slow Thinking, most everyday decisions are quick, intuitive and driven by feeling, with reasons added afterwards. That's why the feeling a product creates matters so much: it shapes whether people trust it, enjoy it and come back.

Colour

Colour psychology looks at how colours influence emotions, behaviour and perception. Associations vary between cultures and contexts, but some are widely shared: red can signal energy, excitement or urgency, while blue often suggests calm and trust.

McDonald's uses red and yellow throughout its branding. Red is energetic and attention-grabbing; yellow feels warm and cheerful, and is visible from far away. Together they suit a brand built on speed, fun and quick decisions.

Typography

Typography is the art of arranging type so that it's legible, readable and appealing. It covers the choice of typeface, size, line length, spacing and alignment, and it sets the tone of a design before anyone reads a word. Serif typefaces often feel traditional and reliable; sans-serif typefaces feel modern and clean. (You'll go deeper in the UI Deck lecture on Typeface.)

Apple uses its own typeface, San Francisco, across its products and marketing. Clean, modern and highly legible, it matches the brand's values of simplicity and innovation, and its consistent use makes every Apple screen feel like part of one family.

Brand and identity

A brand identity is the personality of a company or product, expressed through its logo, colours, typography, imagery and language. A strong identity builds recognition and trust, and creates an emotional connection with the audience.

Nike's swoosh, designed by Carolyn Davidson in 1971, suggests movement and speed. Together with the "Just Do It" slogan, used consistently for decades, it creates a brand people feel something about, not just recognise.

Why it matters for UX

The feeling a product creates decides whether people trust it, enjoy it and come back. Colour, type and brand shape that feeling before users read a word, and good emotional design works at all three of Norman's levels: how it looks, how it works and what it means.

Try it: feel the difference (20 minutes)

  1. Take one screen from your current project.
  2. Write down three words you want users to feel when they see it, for example calm, safe, simple.
  3. Make two versions: change only the colours and typeface to create the opposite feeling in the second.
  4. Show both to a classmate without explaining, and ask for three words each. Did they match your intent?

Key takeaways

  • People respond to design at three levels: visceral (looks), behavioural (use) and reflective (meaning).
  • Decisions are often emotional first and rational second.
  • Colour, typography and brand identity set the feeling of a product before users read a word.
