The company that had everything

For most of the 20th century, Kodak was photography: film, cameras and printing.

"A Kodak moment" became an everyday phrase. At its peak in the late 1990s, the company was worth around $30 billion.

UX Deck 24 · The Death of Kodak2 / 14

In 1975, a young Kodak engineer called Steven Sasson built the first digital camera.

UX Deck 24 · The Death of Kodak3 / 14

Kodak saw the threat

Digital photos don't need film. And film was where Kodak made its money.

So the managers protected the business they had instead of building the one that was coming.

Meanwhile

  • 2000s Digital cameras take over from film

Kodak didn't lack the technology. It had it first.

It lacked the willingness to listen to how people's behaviour was changing, and to adapt.

Kodak wasn't alone.

Markets that moved

Telecoms watched messaging move to WhatsApp and Instagram.

Video rental, travel agencies, music labels, bookshops and banks all saw their markets change, or vanish, with the internet.

The companies built around one specialised way of doing things struggled most.

Adaptation over specialisation.

Watch how customers behave, test ideas quickly and change direction before the market does it for you.

Adapt or disappear?

For each company, decide: did it adapt, or did it make Kodak's mistake? What did it do differently?

  1. Blockbuster rented films from thousands of high-street shops
  2. Netflix started by posting DVDs to people's homes
  3. Fujifilm made most of its money from photographic film, just like Kodak
  4. Nokia was the world's biggest mobile phone maker in the 2000s

Then: which industry do you think is next?

UX Deck 24 · The Death of Kodak13 / 14

Check yourself

  1. Blockbuster: Kodak's mistake. It kept its shops and late fees while viewing moved online, and filed for bankruptcy in 2010
  2. Netflix: adapted. It started streaming in 2007 and later made its own shows, moving away from its own successful DVD business
  3. Fujifilm: adapted. It turned its chemistry know-how into new businesses such as medical imaging and cosmetics
  4. Nokia: Kodak's mistake. It was slow to respond to touchscreen smartphones and sold its phone business to Microsoft in 2014

The pattern: the survivors followed their customers, even when it meant competing with themselves.
