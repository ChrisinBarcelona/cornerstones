Where it began

Gestalt psychology began in Germany in the early 1910s with Max Wertheimer, working with Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka.

Wertheimer wanted to know why we see movement in a series of still images, and shapes where there are only separate marks.

UX Deck 07 · Gestalt and Perception2 / 13

We see the whole first

The brain is wired to find order and patterns in chaos.

Gestalt is German for "shape" or "form": we organise separate marks into unified wholes.

Six principles

  • Proximity Things close together seem to belong together
  • Similarity Things that look alike seem related
  • Closure We complete incomplete shapes in our minds
  • Continuity We follow lines in the smoothest direction
  • Common region Things inside a boundary seem grouped
  • Figure and ground We separate an object from its background

Closure in advertising

There's no bottle in this advert, only buildings.

Your brain draws the bottle anyway. These principles became the bedrock of the Laws of UX.

Depth and shadow

UI borrows from the natural world. A shadow tells the brain a card sits above the page, so it can be picked up or pressed.

Google's Material Design team tested real lighting to get its icons' shadows right.

Negative space

The empty areas in a design. It's figure and ground at work.

Chanel puts one bottle in a sea of white. All your attention goes to the product, and simplicity reads as luxury.

Let it breathe

All copy and content needs space around it.

In code, that's padding inside the border and margin outside it.

Visual hierarchy

Arrange elements to guide the eye to the most important thing first.

Size, colour, contrast, alignment and spacing set the order: big bold headlines, then images, then smaller text.

UX Deck 07 · Gestalt and Perception11 / 13

Take the Times apart

Find a group made by proximity, one by similarity and one by common region.

Then: what does your eye land on first, second and third?

UX Deck 07 · Gestalt and Perception12 / 13

Check yourself

  • Proximity: each headline sits tight against its summary and "6 MIN READ", so they read as one story
  • Similarity: every "MIN READ" label is small grey capitals; the menu items all match, so they read as one set
  • Common region: the two blue buttons, Try 7 days free and Log in; the thin rules boxing off each section
  • Hierarchy: usually the masthead, then the lead photo and headline, then the tree lobster. Large, high-contrast and high on the page wins
