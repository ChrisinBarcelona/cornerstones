Data-driven design

Throughout your sprint, you gather evidence and cycle it back into the design.

That's the great divide between top-down guesswork and design thinking.

UX Deck · Data2 / 13

Data can't predict the future.

But it lets you predict what may happen with greater accuracy. Not certainty: less risk.

UX Deck · Data3 / 13

Start with what you want to know

Every piece of research begins with a question: what don't we know about our users?

A research question is simply an extension of what you wish to learn.

Your methods, your problem statement and your tests all grow from that one question.

UX Deck · Data4 / 13

Two kinds of data

  • Quantitative Numbers: analytics, surveys, A/B tests. It tells you what is happening
  • Qualitative Words and behaviour: interviews, usability tests. It tells you why
  • You need both Numbers find the pattern; people explain it
UX Deck · Data5 / 13

Bad data

Two data points don't make a trend.

Don't see a correlation before it's there. Be rigorous in testing your own assumptions.

UX Deck · Data6 / 13

Fixed needs, negotiated wants

Everyone has the same fundamental needs. That's fixed.

After that, needs become wants, and wants are negotiated: shaped by culture, timing and circumstance.

UX Deck · Data7 / 13

In the 1970s, Jennifer was the most popular girl's name in the United States, year after year.

People are all different, and yet they behave in remarkably similar ways.

UX Deck · Data8 / 13

Groups behave alike

That's why UX designers group users into personas: groups tend to behave the same way in the same circumstances.

Expect people to use your product the same way, for the same reasons. Don't be surprised when they don't.

And ask not only who the user is, but when.

The cost of assuming

In 2017, Pepsi's "Live for Now" advert showed Kendall Jenner ending a protest by handing a police officer a can of Pepsi.

It was attacked for trivialising protest movements. Pepsi pulled it and apologised within a day.

UX Deck · Data10 / 13

It was made by Pepsi's own in-house studio, and nobody stopped it.

Never design in a silo. Talk to people, test and get your work reviewed.

What, why or wrong?

Is each quantitative, qualitative, or a bad use of data?

  1. 68% of users leave at the payment screen
  2. In five interviews, three people said they didn't trust the payment page
  3. "Downloads went from 1 to 2 yesterday, so we'll have 1,000 by next month"
  4. "Everyone on our team loves dark mode, so our users will too"
  5. "People who use the app at night spend more, so night-time makes people spend"
UX Deck · Data12 / 13

Check yourself

  1. Quantitative: it tells you what is happening, and where
  2. Qualitative: it suggests why. Together with 1, you have a pattern and a reason
  3. Bad data: extrapolating from two points
  4. Bad data: an assumption. Your team isn't your users
  5. Bad data: correlation isn't causation. Maybe night users are simply your most loyal ones
