Don Norman

A cognitive scientist who brought user-centred design into the mainstream.

In 1993 he became Apple's User Experience Architect, and he wrote The Design of Everyday Things (1988).

Four principles

  • Affordance What an object lets you do
  • Signifiers Signs that show where and how to act
  • Mapping How controls relate to their results
  • Feedback What tells you an action worked

Affordance

What an object lets you do. A chair affords sitting. A button affords pressing.

Using it should take little thought. The number one problem in UI is nearly always a lack of clarity about what a product actually does.

UX Deck 03 · The Father of UX5 / 12

Signifiers

The signs that show where the action happens and how to do it.

A flat plate on a door says push. A handle says pull. On screen: an underline, a shadow, a chevron, a label.

UX Deck 03 · The Father of UX6 / 12

Mapping

The relationship between a control and its result.

A cooker whose knobs are laid out in the same pattern as its rings needs no labels. A slider that moves up for more matches what people expect.

UX Deck 03 · The Father of UX7 / 12

Feedback

Communicating the result of an action, straight away.

It's the dialogue between person and computer: a tick when a form saves, a sound when a payment goes through. An informed user is a happy user.

Norman wrote a whole book on how design makes us feel.

Which principle fails?

  1. A door with a big pull handle that only opens if you push it
  2. Four hob rings in a square, with four knobs in a straight line
  3. You tap Send and nothing changes, so you tap it three more times
  4. A grey rectangle with no label, shadow or icon. Is it a button?
  5. An app's home screen that never says what the app is for

Then: how would you fix each one?

UX Deck 03 · The Father of UX11 / 12

Check yourself

  1. Signifier: the handle says pull. Use a flat push plate
  2. Mapping: lay the knobs out in the same square as the rings
  3. Feedback: show "Sending…", then a tick
  4. Signifier: give it a label, contrast or shadow so it looks pressable
  5. Affordance: make clear what the product lets you do, in one line, on the first screen
