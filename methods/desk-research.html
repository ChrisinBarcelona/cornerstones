👋Context

Before you interview a single person, someone, somewhere, has probably studied part of your problem already. Desk research, also called secondary research (or a literature review in academia), means gathering existing findings and information before you start your own research.

Nielsen Norman Group calls it "UX learning right at your desk". It helps you understand the problem space, see what similar projects found, spot best practices and trends, and focus the scope of your own research, often saving a lot of time and money. For a wicked problem it is essential: the causes, statistics and actors are usually well documented by researchers, NGOs and governments.

But it is not a substitute for primary research. Desk research tells you what is known in general. Your interviews, surveys and site visits tell you about your users. Do both.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Find credible internal and external sources about your problem space.
  • Judge each source for reliability, bias and fit with your users.
  • Summarise what is already known, with references.
  • Refine your problem statement and decide what your own research still needs to find out.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Write the question (10 min). In one or two sentences, describe the problem you're investigating, e.g. "Why do young adults in Barcelona waste food at home?"
  2. Find sources (45 min). Aim for 6–10, mixing:
    • Internal: your earlier research, journey maps, blueprints, workshop outputs, the team's notes.
    • External: academic papers (Google Scholar), reports from NGOs, governments and universities, open data, reputable industry studies, and NN/g articles.
  3. Judge each source (20 min). For each one, note: Who wrote it, and why? Is it based on an actual study, and does it explain its methods and limitations? How recent is it? Were the people studied like your users?
  4. Capture findings (30 min). One finding per sticky note with its source, e.g. "A third of food bought is thrown away (source, year)".
  5. Synthesise (20 min). Group the findings into themes (see Affinity Mapping) and mark where sources agree or contradict each other.
  6. Refine (15 min). Update your problem statement and list the gaps: what's unknown, or unknown about your users, becomes the focus of your primary research.

Tips
  • Prefer peer-reviewed research and official statistics. A missing methods section is a red flag.
  • Blog posts are often opinion. Ask: is this based on an actual study?
  • Check the audience. Findings about one group may not apply to another, but they can still give you hypotheses to test.
  • Always record where each finding came from, so you can cite it in your case study.
  • If you use AI to find sources, open and read every one. AI can invent references.
AI Assist

Ask Claude: "What kinds of organisations or researchers would study [your problem]? Suggest search terms I can use in Google Scholar." Use it to find where to look, then read the real sources yourself.

Summary

Desk research gathers what is already known about a problem before you start your own research. Done critically, with credible sources and a clear record of where each finding comes from, it sharpens your problem statement, keeps your scope focused and shows exactly what your primary research still needs to discover.
