UX Strategy is only as good as your ability to communicate it.

👋Context

A checkpoint presentation is a short, informal update in the middle of a sprint. It brings the room up to speed with your progress and gives everyone else a chance to challenge it. The questions and push-back (the rebuttal) are the point: a good challenge today saves you a wasted week later.

It covers your key milestones, not the full sprint. Nobody needs every sticky note. They need to know what you set out to find out, what you've learned so far and where you're heading next. Keep it under five minutes.

A checkpoint is not the same as a General Audience Presentation. These are two separate types of presentation:

  • A checkpoint is a working update for people who know the brief. It's built to be questioned.
  • A general audience presentation tells the story of your journey to people who weren't in the room, in your own voice.

Three things to cover

Whatever you show, keep it focused on three things:

  1. What you wanted to know.
  2. What you gained an understanding of.
  3. Which ideas and concepts remain to be tested and taken forward.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Bring the room up to speed in under five minutes, focused on key milestones.
  • Lead with insights, backed by user quotes, relevant points or major data points.
  • Mention your research methods only for the insights they produced.
  • Name what's still unearthed, and take a challenge from the room to sharpen your next steps.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Open with your question 🔍. What did you set out to find out? Let your personal curiosity come through: say in one sentence why this problem matters to you.
  2. Pick your key milestones 🏁. Choose two or three moments where you learned something. Leave the rest out.
  3. Lead with insights 💡. Keep it mainly about insights. For each milestone, say the insight first, then the evidence: a user quote, a relevant point or a major data point.
  4. Mention methods, briefly 🛠️. Name the research method behind each insight ("from five interviews…"), but limit the discussion to the key insights it produced, not how you ran it.
  5. Steps forward 🧭. What do you feel is still unearthed? What needs discovering next, and which ideas need testing?
  6. Rehearse against the clock ⏱️. Run it once out loud. If it's over five minutes, cut a milestone, not the insights.
  7. Invite the rebuttal 🥊. Finish by asking the room to challenge you: "What would you push back on?" Write down every challenge.
Extra Notes

Presenting UX work: five simple best practices

  • Lead with the insight. Put the most important finding first and the detail after, like the inverted pyramid Nielsen Norman Group recommends for writing. If the room only remembers one thing, make sure it's the insight.
  • Tell a story. Raw data rarely convinces anyone on its own. A narrative connects your data to why people should care (Nielsen Norman Group).
  • Know your audience. Use their language and answer what they care about before they have to ask (Nielsen Norman Group).
  • Show the evidence. Ground every claim in research: direct user quotes, observed behaviour and the numbers that matter. Pair a statistic with a short anecdote so people can picture it.
  • End with next steps. A good readout moves people to action, not just acknowledgment. Say what you'll test next and what you need from the room.
Template
  • 🔍 What I wanted to know: the question, and why I'm curious
  • 🏁 Key milestones: two or three, no more
  • 💡 Insights: one per milestone, each with a quote, point or data point
  • 🛠️ Methods: named in one line, only for the insights they produced
  • 🧭 Steps forward: what's still unearthed, and what to test next
  • 🥊 Rebuttal: the challenges from the room
  • ⏱️ Under five minutes
Tips
  • One slide per milestone is plenty.
  • Read quotes word for word, and say who said them ("a regular customer, 30s").
  • One big number beats a chart nobody can read from the back of the room.
  • During the rebuttal, don't defend: listen, say thanks and write it down. Decide what to do with it later.
  • If you don't know what comes next, say so. That's exactly what the room can help with.
