👋Context

By now you have more ideas and features than you can possibly build. You need a fair way to choose.

You can look at any idea through three lenses to find the innovation sweet spot: where people want it, you can build it and it makes financial sense. This is the Desirability, Feasibility, Viability (DVF) framework, developed by IDEO as part of design thinking and still widely used across the industry.

  1. Desirable: do people want it? The product needs to solve a real problem or meet a genuine need.
  2. Feasible: can we build it? We must have the technical know-how to bring it to market.
  3. Viable: should we build it? The product must contribute to our long-term success and build an advantage.

Great innovation sits where all three overlap. To choose between ideas, turn the three lenses into a scorecard: Nielsen Norman Group describes DVF as a prioritisation method that scores each idea against the three criteria and adds up the totals. The score is a starting point for discussion, not the final answer.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Look at an idea through the three lenses: desirable, feasible and viable.
  • Score a list of ideas or features for desirability, feasibility and viability.
  • Back each score with evidence from research, the team or the business model.
  • Rank ideas and discuss the results as a team.
  • Agree which ideas go into the next prototype.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Answer the DVF questions. Before you score anything, answer these questions about your product as a team, in writing. Mark every answer you can back with research, and every one that's still a guess.

    Desirable: do people want it? The product needs to solve a real problem or meet a genuine need.

    • Who is our target user?
    • What is the core problem we are trying to solve for them?
    • Is this a problem they actively care about solving?
    • Why is our solution better?
    • Are users willing to change their behaviour to adopt ours?

    Feasible: can we build it? We must have the technical know-how to bring it to market.

    • Do we have the tech required to build this? If not, can we acquire or develop it?
    • Does our team have the necessary skills?
    • How much time will it take to make it viable?
    • What are the operational needs for maintaining this product?
    • Can we scale the tech reliably as the user base grows?

    Viable: should we build it? The product must contribute to our long-term success and build an advantage.

    • Does this product align with our strategy?
    • What is our monetisation model? (e.g. subscription, one-time purchase, freemium)
    • Are users willing to pay for this solution?
    • What are the economics? (e.g. profit, ROI, customer lifetime value)
    • How does this position us in the market?

  2. List the ideas. Take 5–10 ideas or features from your ideation or affinity map. Write each as a short phrase.

  3. Build the scorecard. A table with one row per idea and columns for Desirability, Feasibility, Viability and Total.
  4. Score 1–10. For each idea, score each criterion and write one line of evidence for the score, using your answers to the questions above.
  5. Add up and rank. Sort from highest to lowest total.
  6. Discuss and adjust. Does the ranking feel right? Move items if the team can justify it. If one criterion matters more for your project, weight it (e.g. desirability ×2).
  7. Decide. Choose the top ideas to take into your prototype, and note which ones you are parking.

Tips
  • Score as informed as possible. Bring your research, someone who knows the tech and your Business Model Canvas.
  • An idea that only scores on one lens isn't ready: desirable but not viable is a wish, feasible but not desirable is a gadget.
  • Score each criterion across all ideas before moving to the next. It keeps the scale consistent.
  • A low feasibility score isn't always a "no": maybe it's "not yet".
  • Don't let the maths decide for you. The conversation is the point.

Summary

DVF analysis looks at every idea through three lenses: is it desirable (do people want it?), feasible (can we build it?) and viable (should we build it?). Great innovation sits in the sweet spot where all three meet. Scoring each idea on the three lenses, then discussing the ranking, turns a long wish list into a short, defensible plan.
