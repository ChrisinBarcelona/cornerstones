👋Context

You can read about a market, a bakery queue or a hospital waiting room, but you will only feel it by standing in it. A site visit is a short piece of field research: you go to the environment your users live and work in and watch what really happens there.

Nielsen Norman Group's point about field studies is simple: context reveals things you would never think to ask about. People forget their workarounds, the noise, the interruptions, the sticky note on the till. They do not forget them on purpose; they simply stop noticing. You will notice, because it is all new to you.

A site visit is not an interview and it is not a test. Your main job is to observe, to pick up on cues, and to get a feel for the subject matter before you start designing.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Experience the environment your users are in, first hand.
  • Spot cues that users would never mention: workarounds, interruptions, signage, tools and emotions.
  • Come back with evidence (notes, sketches, photos) rather than assumptions.
  • Turn what you saw into better research questions for the rest of the sprint.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Prepare (10 min). Write down two or three things you want to learn, taken from your Research Questions. Decide where you will go and when the place is at its most typical. Ask permission if it is a private space.
  2. Observe quietly (30–45 min). Find a spot where you can see without getting in the way. Look for:
    • The environment: noise, light, crowding, queues, signage.
    • Artefacts: the objects, screens, paper and tools people use, and the ones they make for themselves.
    • Workarounds: extra steps, shortcuts and anything done "the wrong way" that works.
    • Interruptions: what breaks people's flow and how they recover.
    • Emotion: where people look relaxed, rushed, confused or pleased.
  3. Capture. Write notes, sketch layouts and take photos of places and objects (never photograph people without their consent).
  4. Talk to one or two people (optional). If the moment is right, ask a short open question such as "What's the hardest part of this for you?" Don't lead them.
  5. Debrief straight away (15 min). While it is fresh, write your top five observations, one surprise, and three new questions you now want to answer.
Tips
  • Observe first, ask later. Asking questions too early changes what people do.
  • Write down what you see, not what you think it means. Interpretation comes in the debrief.
  • Go at a typical time. A café at 3 p.m. on a Tuesday is not the café at 9 a.m. on a Monday.
  • Go in pairs if you can: one watches, one writes.
  • Be respectful and invisible. You are a guest in someone else's space.

Summary

A site visit puts you in your users' world. By watching quietly and noting the environment, the tools, the workarounds and the emotions, you pick up cues that no survey or interview would give you. It builds empathy, grounds the sprint in reality and sharpens the questions you ask next.
