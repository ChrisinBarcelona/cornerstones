👋Context

We test the prototype with at least five people, and every session has two roles: the facilitator, who leads the conversation, and the notetaker, who records it (see Notetaker Format). This page is about the facilitator.

The facilitator's job is to make the participant comfortable enough to be open, honest and critical, then to step back. People can easily read a test as a test of their ability, so the most important message is: we are testing the prototype, not you. After that, you are a host, not a teacher. You need the participant to become a narrator.

The session runs in the order of your script (see Create your Script):

  1. Welcome the participant.
  2. Invite them to open up about themselves.
  3. Discover their context in relation to the problem.
  4. Introduce the prototype.
  5. Give them the objective.
  6. Ask them to think out loud, and gently nudge without telling.
  7. Ask for their impression at the end.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Run a test session that feels relaxed and informal.
  • Stay neutral: introduce the prototype in a way that invites criticism.
  • Get participants to think aloud, and prompt them with open questions.
  • Close with a debrief that reveals why people felt the way they did.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Welcome (2 min). Warm greeting, small talk, a smile. Say clearly that you are testing the prototype, not them, and that getting stuck helps you find problems.
  2. Invite them to open up (3 min). Ask about their job, interests and life. Listen, be interested, find common ground. Watch for verbal and non-verbal cues.
  3. Discover their context (5 min). Ask about the problem space: have they faced this problem? How do they solve it now? Are they satisfied? Do they feel the pain points from your research?
  4. Introduce the prototype (2 min). Ask permission to show it. Explain that it's a mock-up and some buttons won't work. Distance yourself: "I didn't build this, so you won't hurt my feelings." This reduces people's tendency to be polite rather than honest.
  1. Give the objective. Read the task as a scenario: "Imagine you want to… in order to… How would you go about it?" If they ask where to go, repeat the task and say "Start wherever you think you might." Never give the answer: prompts are clues, and it's no fun when you give the answer away.
  1. Keep them thinking aloud. Listen far more than you speak. If they go quiet, use open prompts:
    • "What are your thoughts on that?"
    • "How did you feel about that?"
    • "Tell me more about what you're looking for."
    • "What made you choose that option?"
  1. Debrief (5 min). Now you may ask direct questions about what you want to know. Unlike metrics, this is where you find out why. Finish with "If you had three magic wishes to improve this, what would they be?", thank them and see them out.
Tips
  • Smile, relax and keep everything informal; your nerves are contagious.
  • Silence is a tool. Count to five before you rescue someone.
  • If they ask "Is this right?", answer with "What do you think?"
  • Don't explain the design or defend it. If you hear yourself saying "What we meant was…", stop.
  • Stick to the script's order so sessions are comparable.
AI Assist

Before your sessions, ask Claude to role-play a participant who is shy, then one who keeps asking for help. Practise staying neutral with both.

Checklist

☐ Welcomed the participant and said we're testing the prototype, not them

☐ Explored their context before showing the prototype

☐ Introduced the prototype neutrally and asked them to think aloud

☐ Gave tasks as goals and never gave the answer

☐ Ran a debrief, including the three magic wishes

Summary

The facilitator sets the tone of a test. A warm welcome, a neutral introduction and goal-based tasks get people talking; open prompts and patient silence keep them thinking aloud; and a short debrief uncovers why they felt the way they did.
