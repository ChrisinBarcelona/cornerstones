👋Context

Before you design screens, you need to know which screens exist and where they live. A sitemap is a diagram of how a site's or app's content is organised: a hierarchy of boxes (pages or content) joined by lines (their relationships).

Nielsen Norman Group makes an important distinction: a sitemap is not the information architecture itself. The IA is the thinking: how content is grouped, labelled and found, often informed by research like card sorting and tree testing. The sitemap is the artifact that makes that thinking visible and shareable. So the IA comes first, and the sitemap follows.

Sitemaps are planning tools for the team, not something users see. They are great for spotting missing pages, duplicated content and sections that are too deep or too crowded. In Portfolio Week, you'll map your own portfolio website before you build it.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Understand the difference between information architecture and a sitemap.
  • Group and label content into a clear hierarchy, two or three levels deep.
  • Draw a sitemap with consistent notation that the team can read at a glance.
  • Use the sitemap to spot gaps and decide which pages to design first.

🛠️Main Exercise

We recommend Octopus.do, a free visual sitemap builder: you add pages as cards, nest them into levels and even sketch the content blocks of each page, then export the result. FigJam or Miro work too.

  1. List all the content. Every page, section and piece of content your site needs. For a portfolio: home, about, each case study, contact, CV…
  2. Group it. Cluster similar content together (you can run a quick card sort with a classmate). Name each group with the label a user would expect.
  3. Decide the levels. Level 1 = main navigation. Level 2 = pages inside each section. Level 3 = detail pages. Keep it as flat as makes sense.
  4. Draw it. Home at the top, then one box per page, joined by lines. Use one colour per level.
  5. Annotate. Mark which boxes are real pages and which are just categories; add utility pages (search, login, legal) to the side.
  6. Check it. Can every page be reached? Is anything orphaned, duplicated or buried too deep? Walk a classmate through a task ("Find Chris's second case study") using only the map.
Template
  • 🟦 Level 1: main navigation
  • 🟩 Level 2: section pages
  • 🟨 Level 3: detail pages
  • ⬜ Utility pages (search, contact, legal), off to the side
Tips
  • Keep it high level: only the detail the team needs to discuss and decide.
  • Use your users' words for labels, not internal jargon.
  • Deep hierarchies hide content; very flat ones overwhelm. Aim for a balance.
  • A sitemap can't fix a weak IA. If the groups don't make sense, go back and re-sort.
  • Expect it to change as you learn more.

Summary

A sitemap is the visual record of your information architecture: a hierarchy of pages and how they connect. It helps the team agree on structure, spot missing or duplicated content, and plan which pages to design, before anyone opens a wireframe.
