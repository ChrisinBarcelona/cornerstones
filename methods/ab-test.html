👋Context

Sometimes the team has two good options and can't agree. An A/B test settles it with evidence instead of opinion.

Nielsen Norman Group defines A/B testing as a quantitative method: two versions of a design run on live traffic, the original (A, the control) and a variation (B), and you measure which one performs better on a predetermined metric, such as sign-ups or completed checkouts. Done properly, it needs a clear hypothesis, one change at a time, and thousands of users. And it tells you what happened, never why.

On a bootcamp project you don't have live traffic, so you'll run the prototype version: show two variants of the same screen to participants and compare how they perform and which they prefer. NN/g calls the preference part a preference test: participants compare two or three variations and choose one, and you ask them to explain their choice. You get a small, qualitative signal, plus the reasons behind it.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Write a clear hypothesis for one design change.
  • Build two variants that differ in a single, noticeable way.
  • Compare the variants on task success and preference, and capture the reasons.
  • Understand the difference between a real A/B test and a prototype preference test.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Pick one decision. E.g. the layout of a product card, a button label, or the order of a sign-up flow.
  2. Write a hypothesis. "If we , then more users will , because ___."
  3. Build A and B. Duplicate the frame in Figma and change only one thing. Make the difference big enough that a non-designer would notice it.
  4. Test with at least 5 people each. Either:
    • Split: half your participants do the task on A, half on B. Record success, time and errors.
    • Preference: show each participant both (swap the order between people), then ask "Which would you choose?" and "Why?"
  5. Ask for reactions. Give participants a short list of words (e.g. trustworthy, confusing, modern, cluttered) and ask them to pick three for each version.
  6. Compare and decide. Put the results side by side: success, preference, reasons, words. Was your hypothesis right? Choose a version, or combine the best of both.

Tips
  • One change only. Change two things and you won't know which made the difference.
  • Swap the order. People tend to favour whatever they see first, or last.
  • Five people is a signal, not proof. Say so in your case study.
  • Preference isn't performance. People sometimes prefer the version they did worse on: report both.
  • On a live product with real traffic, use a proper A/B tool and run it for at least one to two weeks.

Summary

An A/B test compares two versions of a design to see which works better. On live products it is a quantitative test of behaviour; in a sprint, a prototype version with split tasks and a preference test gives a quick signal plus the reasons behind it. Either way: one change, a clear hypothesis, and evidence instead of opinion.
