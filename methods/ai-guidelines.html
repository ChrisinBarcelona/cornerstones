👋Context

AI is now part of every stage of a UX sprint: synthesising research, drafting personas, generating ideas, building prototypes. Used well, it saves hours. Used badly, it produces confident, generic, sometimes wrong work, and puts your name on it.

This method gives you two frameworks for writing prompts, and one rule that matters more than either.

The rule: human in the loop

Human in the loop means a person stays inside the process: setting the goal, checking every output and making every decision that matters. For UX, that's not optional. You are the designer; AI is the assistant.

  • You command, AI assists. You decide what problem to solve, for whom and why. AI helps you get there faster.
  • AI drafts, you decide. Treat every output as a first draft from a fast, keen intern who has never met your users.
  • Never outsource empathy. AI hasn't spoken to your users. Interviews, observation and testing stay human.
  • Check everything. AI can invent quotes, statistics and sources. If you can't verify it, don't use it.
  • Own the result. If it goes in your case study, you must be able to explain and defend every part of it.

Framework 1: C.A.R.E.

Nielsen Norman Group's CARE framework structures a single prompt:

  • C – Context: the situation, the project and who you are. "I'm a UX designer researching how tourists use Barceloneta beach."
  • A – Ask: exactly what you want. "Group these 40 interview notes into themes."
  • R – Rules: the constraints. "Use only what's in the notes. Quote participants. Max 6 themes."
  • E – Examples: show what good looks like. "Format each theme like this: …"

Framework 2: T.C.R.E.I.

Google's Prompting Essentials teaches a five-step loop (remembered as "Tiny Crabs Ride Enormous Iguanas"):

  • T – Task: what you want, including a persona and the output format.
  • C – Context: the background the AI needs.
  • R – References: examples or material to work from.
  • E – Evaluate: check the output. Is it accurate, relevant and what you asked for?
  • I – Iterate: refine the prompt and try again until it is.

Same but different

Both frameworks agree on the basics: say what you want (Ask / Task), give the background (Context) and show examples (Examples / References).

The difference is where they stop. CARE is about writing one strong prompt, with rules built in up front. T.C.R.E.I. carries on after the answer arrives: it makes evaluating and iterating part of the method, which is human in the loop written into the framework itself.

C.A.R.E. T.C.R.E.I.
Best for One well-built prompt A back-and-forth to get to a great result
Strength Rules and examples keep output on track first time Builds checking and refining into the habit
Watch out Easy to accept the first answer Can take longer for simple tasks

Use CARE to write the prompt. Use the E and I of T.C.R.E.I. every time you read the answer.

🎯Goal / Learning Outcome

  • Explain what "human in the loop" means and why UX needs it.
  • Write a prompt with the CARE framework.
  • Evaluate and iterate on an output with T.C.R.E.I.
  • Decide which parts of a sprint AI can help with, and which must stay human.

🛠️Main Exercise

  1. Pick a real task from your sprint, for example clustering interview notes or drafting survey questions.
  2. Write a CARE prompt. Label each part (C, A, R, E) so you can see it's complete.
  3. Run it in Claude or another assistant.
  4. Evaluate the output against your research. Highlight anything invented, generic or wrong.
  5. Iterate at least twice, changing one thing in the prompt each time. Note what improved.
  6. Decide what you keep, what you change and what you throw away, and write one line on why.
  7. Map your sprint. In two columns, list the tasks AI may help with, and the tasks that stay human (interviews, decisions, testing with users).
Template
  • 🧭 Context: …
  • 🎯 Ask / Task: …
  • 📏 Rules: …
  • 🖼️ Examples / References: …
  • 🔍 Evaluate: what's accurate, what's invented, what's generic?
  • 🔁 Iterate: what I changed, and what improved
  • 🙋 Human decision: what I kept, and why
Tips
  • Never paste personal data from participants into an AI tool without their consent. Anonymise first.
  • Ask the AI to show its sources or quote the material it used, then check them.
  • If you couldn't explain the output to your mentor, you don't understand it well enough to use it.
  • Say in your case study where you used AI and how you checked it. It's a strength, not a confession.

Summary

CARE (Context, Ask, Rules, Examples) helps you write one strong prompt; T.C.R.E.I. (Task, Context, References, Evaluate, Iterate) turns prompting into a loop of checking and refining. Both rest on one rule: in UX the human stays in the loop. You set the goal, you check the output and you make the decisions. AI is the assistant, never the designer.
